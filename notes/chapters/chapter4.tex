\chapter{Cosmology}\label{ch:iv}

\section*{Pre-Relativistic Cosmology and Its Problems}

Long before GR as we know it today was a thing, Cosmology was already an open field of study. Of course, in this first age of Cosmology, studies were based on Newtonian mechanics and the Newtonian theory of gravity.
\par Newtonian Cosmology assumed an infinite, homogeneous, and static Universe, which was also eternal and responded to Euclidean geometry. Not surprisingly, under this set of un-reasonable hypothesis\footnote{The only reasonable assumption was for the Universe to be homogeneous and, only at times, static. The rest of the hypothesis were arguable at best.}, problems began to sprout overnight.
\par Consider for example the so called \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olbers%27_paradox}{Olber's paradox} (1826). Under the assumptions of Newtonian Cosmology, Olber's paradox would implicate an always-bright sky, while we have empirical evidence it isn't. In fact, the total luminosity of a set of stars with solar luminosity $L_{\odot}$ within a sphere of radius $R$
\begin{equation*}
    \phi_{s} = \int_{0}^{R} \frac{L_{\odot}}{4\pi r^{2}}n_{s}4\pi r^{2}\diff{r}
\end{equation*}
will tend to infinity once $R\to \infty$. That is because, under the hypothesis of homogeneity, the surface density of stars $n_{s}$ cannot depened on the spatial coordinates.
\par Olber will then try to solve his own paradox, claiming it to be solved by dust in the ISM (read: Interstellar Medium). But, if you think about it, it really doesn't solve anything: Sooner or later, the dust itself will get into thermodynamical equilibrium with the radiation, and at that point dust will begin to emit on its own.
\par A second solution was given in 1872 from Zolbnev, who assumed that the Universe is \emph{closed} with a \emph{positive curvature}. The Universe would then be finite, and the assumption of homogeneity wouldn't let the integral above diverge.
\par However, solved a problem, there's always another one\footnote{"Always two there are."} peeking around the corner. In this particular case it's the problem of gravitational stability. In fact, it's evident that local instabilities in the gravitational potential must have occurred at some point in time through fluctuations of density, leading to collapses and star formation epochs. As I said, the problem is mainly local, and instabilities are generally balanced on the larger scales.
\par Bentley then showed that perturbations on spatial scale $r$ have, under the assumption of homogeneity, an associated potential $\propto r^{2}$ and an associated acceleration $\propto r$, which leads to unstable equilibrium.
\par This problem is however partially mitigated by Seelinger (1895), who assumes gravity to be \emph{shielded} on the large scales
\begin{equation*}
    \phi = G\int \frac{\rho}{r}\,\text{d}^{3}x \to \phi = G\int \frac{\rho}{r}e^{-\lambda r}\,\text{d}^{3}x
\end{equation*}
where the exponential term is an absorption/shielding factor. This correction will thus bring about a modification of Poisson's equation
\begin{equation*}
    \nabla^{2}\phi = 4\pi G \rho \to \nabla^{2}\phi -\lambda\phi = 4\pi G\rho
\end{equation*}
Much expectedly, quite a lot changed since 1917, when relativistic cosmology was officially born \cite{1917SPAW.......142E}. Einstein's GR was based on three main principles:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item (Weak) Equivalence Principle: Inertial acceleration and gravitational acceleration are proportional to each other. Hence all free-falling systems are locally equivalent to an inertial system;
    \item General Covariance Principle: When written in covariant form, the laws of Physics are the same in all reference frame;
    \item Mach's Principle\footnote{This one is a bit "philosophical", but it's interesteing to at least mention it.} The momentum of all bodies is determined by all the masses in the Universe. Alternatively, the only things with physical sense are those that are measurable.
\end{enumerate}
Only a year prior to Einstein's 1917 article, Schwarzschild had already found a solution to Einstein's celebrated equation under the assumption of a single spherical body in an otherwise empty space. Such solution, that is nowadays known as Schwarzschild's metric, has to the perk to come down to the standard spacetime Minkowski's metric at infinite distance from the object. This, of course, felt like a big problem for Mach's philosophical principle.
\par Einstein, perhaps conditioned from the beliefs of his time, twisted his theory so to produce a closed, static Universe. To do so, he only had to add a single term to his equation, the Cosmological Constant
\begin{equation}
    G_{\mu\nu} - \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G T_{\mu\nu}
    \label{eq:modified_einsteineq}
\end{equation}
which is actually perfectly valid, even from a theoretical point of view\footnote{I assume the four readers of these notes to be at least familiar with the terms and symbols used. If not, I suggest reading \cite{carroll} or any graduate/postgraduate textbook about GR.}. That said, the interpretation of the $\Lambda$ term was somewhat...elusive.
\par In 1918, Erwin Schrödinger suggested that the Cosmological Constant was equivalent to the energy-momentum tensor of a fluid with negative pressure, and could thus be included in the right-hand side term of the equation.
\par But, as you perhaps know, even Einstein was prone to errors like any other human being. In 1917, Vesto Slipher observed a discrepancy in the observed emission lines of some galaxies (with respect to the expected frequencies). He, however, failed to correlate such discrepancy to the distance of the aforementioned galaxies.
\par It was only when Hubble measured their distance \cite{1926CMWCI.324....1H} through the Cepheid-variables method that cosmologists started to realize that the Universe was expanding rather than being static; the expansion law of the Universe still carries Hubble's name, who was the first to suggest such correlation.
\par Another viable model to describe the Universe is the so-called "Einstein-de Sitter" Universe. This model essentially assumes the Universe to be akin to a non-relativistic fluid for the sake of computing the resulting metric and the properties stemming from it. This model was however abandoned because it produced errors in the estimation of the age of the Universe\footnote{It turned out that some white dwarves, low mass stars that had reached the end of their evolution cycle, would be older than the Universe. I hope you'll agree with me that it would be at least a bit odd.}.

\subsection*{Thermodynamical Considerations on the Big Bang}

The Laws of Thermodynamics speak rather clearly: Energy is (globally) conserved, and Entropy either remains constant or grows. But, if entropy is not growing, the system (i.e. the Universe) doesn't evolve and will meet its end in a rather ominous-sounding way: Thermal Death.
\par If, on the other hand, entropy is allowed to grow, then it is possible for the system to switch to more probable microscopical configurations. So, starting from a low-entropy, extremely ordered state, the Universe would then evolve through thermodynamical means into the messy Universe we see today.

\section{Maximally Symmetric Universes}

\subsection{Isometries and Killing Vectors}

Consider a metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ and a given map of coordinates $x_{\mu}$ (which roughly translates into choosing a given reference frame). The metric will thus transform according to the standard tensor tranformation rule
\begin{equation}
    g_{\mu\nu}'(x') = \parder{x^{\rho}}{x'^{\mu}}\parder{x^{\sigma}}{x'^{\nu}}g_{\rho\sigma}(x)
\end{equation}
We'll say that a particular transformation $x \to x'$ is an \emph{isometry} if 
\begin{equation}
    g_{\rho\sigma}'(y) = g_{\rho\sigma}(y)
\end{equation}
\par If we consider an infinitesimal transformation of the likes
\begin{equation}
    x'^{\mu} = x^{\mu} + \epsilon \xi^{\mu}
\end{equation}
with $|\epsilon| \ll 1$, we can substitute directly into the transformation equation for the metric. Under the request of the transformation being an isometry, we'll obtain
\begin{equation}
    \partial_{\rho}\xi^{\mu}g_{\mu\sigma} \partial_{\sigma}\xi^{\nu}g_{\rho\nu} + \xi^{\mu}\partial_{\mu}g_{\rho\sigma} = 0
\end{equation}
We can then introduce Christoffel's symbols to cast the equation in an equivalent form
\begin{equation}
    \partial_{\rho}\xi_{\sigma} + \partial_{\sigma}\xi_{\rho} - 2\xi_{\mu}\Gamma_{\rho\;\;\;\sigma}^{\;\;\mu} = 0
\end{equation}
The vector $\xi^{\mu}$ will be a \emph{Killing vector} if it satisfies Killing's equation
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\rho}\xi_{\sigma} + \nabla_{\sigma}\xi_{\rho} = 0
\end{equation}
\par If a given spacetime manifold has $n$ spatial dimensions, then the space can have \emph{at most} $n(n+1)/2$ Killing vectors. Such space is called \emph{maximally symmetric}.

\subsection{The Bare Minimum to Begin}

Contemporary cosmological models are based on the idea the the Universe is pretty much the same everywhere, a stance sometimes known as the \emph{Copernican Principle} or \emph{Cosmological principle}. If we take it at face value, such a claim seems crazy, if at all logical: There's little resemblance, say, between the center of the Sun (which is kinda hot) and the desolate cold of the ISM. The Cosmological Principle starts to make a little more sense if we take it to apply only on the very largest scales, where local variations in density are averaged over and magically disappear.
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{img/isotropyhomogeneity.png}
    \caption{The Cosmological Prinicple automatically translates in two very specific requests: Homogeneity and Isotropy.}
    \label{fgr:homoiso}
\end{figure}
\par The Cosmological principle is related to two more mathematically precise properties that we can request our space-time manifold to have: \emph{Isotropy} and \emph{Homogeneity}, which are given a pictorial representation in Fig.\ref{fgr:homoiso}.
\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced,attach boxed title to top center={yshift=-3mm,yshifttext=-1mm},
  colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!75!black,colbacktitle=blue!80!black,
  title=Homogeneity,
  boxed title style={size=small,colframe=blue!50!black}]
  Consider a family of hypersurfaces defined by some parameter $t$ which allows us to foliate the manifold. Given any two points $p$ and $q$ in a manifold $M$, there is an isometry that takes $p$ into $q$. See \cite{carroll}.
\end{tcolorbox}
\par The assumption of Homogeneity essentially requires that the metric is the same throughout the manifold.
\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced,attach boxed title to top center={yshift=-3mm,yshifttext=-1mm},
  colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!75!black,colbacktitle=blue!80!black,
  title=Isotropy,
  boxed title style={size=small,colframe=blue!50!black}]
  A manifold $M$ is isotropic around a point $p$ if, for any two vectors $V$ and $W$ in the tangent space of $M$ to the point $p$ $T_{p}M$, there is an isometry (symmetry) of $M$ such that the \emph{pushforward} of $W$ under the isometry is parallel with $V$ (not pushed forward). See \cite{carroll}.
\end{tcolorbox}
\par Isotropy, instead, states that space looks exactly the same around a given point, no matter in what direction you're looking.
\par It turns out that a manifold that is simultaneously homogeneous and isotropic is also maximally symmetric! In fact the transformations associated to isotropy are the rotations 
\begin{equation}
    x'^{\mu} = R^{\mu}_{\;\;\nu}x^{\nu}
    \label{eq:rotations}
\end{equation}
which generate $n(n-1)/2$ Killing vectors. On the other hand, the transformations associated to homogeneity are the translations
\begin{equation}
    x'^{\mu} = x^{\mu} + \xi^{\mu}
    \label{eq:translations}
\end{equation}
which generate $n$ Killing vectors. The total number of Killing vector is, as expected, exactly $n(n+1)/2$. A spacetime which has a maximally symmetric spatial section must have a special metric that reflects such properties. Let's start from a general metric
\begin{equation}
    \diff{s^{2}} = g_{\mu\nu}\diff{x^{\mu}}\diff{x^{\nu}}
\end{equation}
Clearly, off-diagonal terms like $g_{0i}$ must be vanishing $g_{0i} = 0$, or the request of an isotropic manifold wouldn't be met. This already leaves us with a metric with the following expression
\begin{equation}
    \diff{s^{2}} = -(u_{\mu}\diff{x^{\mu}})^{2} + g_{ij}(t)\diff{x^{i}}\diff{x^{j}}
\end{equation}
where the first term of the right-hand side represents the projection of the displacement along the tangent vector $u^{\mu}$, which is, by virtue of its own definition, perpendicular to the spatial hypersurfaces.
\par Let's focus on the spatial metric $g_{ij}(t)$. Thanks to isotropy, the temporal dependence of the metric has to factor out, so that 
\begin{equation}
    g_{ij}(t) = a^{2}(t)\tilde{g}_{ij}
\end{equation}
where we've introduced the \emph{scale factor} $a(t)$.
\par Taking $u_{\mu} = (1,0,0,0)$, our metric turns out to be
\begin{equation}
    \diff{s^{2}} = -\diff{t^{2}} + a^{2}(t)\tilde{g}_{ij}\diff{x^{i}}\diff{x^{j}}
\end{equation}
\par Let's now consider a $n$-dimensional geometry which we embed into a $n+1$ dimensional space.

\subsubsection*{3-Dimensional Space}

So let's, for example, write down the metric for a 2-dimensional sphere embedded in a 3-dimensional space. The 3-dimensional Euclidean metric is
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = \diff{x_{1}^{2}}+\diff{x_{2}^{2}}+\diff{x_{3}^{2}}
    \label{eq:euclidean_3d}
\end{equation}
where we can define the radius of a given sphere $a$ as
\begin{equation}
    a^{2} = x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}
\end{equation}
which we can implicitly differentiate to obtain an expression for, say, $\diff{x_{3}}$ which we'll plug into (\ref{eq:euclidean_3d})
\begin{equation}
    \diff{x_{3}} = -\frac{x_{1}\diff{x_{1}+x_{2}\diff{x_{2}}}}{\pm (a^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2})^{1/2}}
\end{equation}
so that the line element induced on the 2-sphere is
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = \diff{x_{1}^{2}}+\diff{x_{2}^{2}} + \frac{(x_{1}\diff{x_{1}+x_{2}\diff{x_{2}}})^{2}}{a^{2}-x_{1}^{2}-x_{2}^{2}}
\end{equation}
We can define a vector $\vec{x} = (x_{1}, x_{2})$, so that the line element can be expressed in a more compact fashion
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = \diff{\vec{x}^{\,2}} + \frac{(\vec{x}\cdot\diff{\vec{x}})^{2}}{a^{2}-\vec{x}^{\,2}}
\end{equation}
\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced,attach boxed title to top center={yshift=-3mm,yshifttext=-1mm},
  colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!75!black,colbacktitle=blue!80!black,
  title=Flat Space,
  boxed title style={size=small,colframe=blue!50!black}]
  The case of a flat space with vanishing curvature can be dealt with starting from the metric induced on the sphere, simply imposing $a\to \infty$. Not surprisingly, the resulting metric will be
  $$ \diff{l^{2}} = \diff{\vec{x}^{\,2}} $$
\end{tcolorbox}
Similarly, we can also calculate the induced metric on a 2-dimension hyperbola
\begin{equation}
    a^{2} = x_{1}^{2}+x_{2}^{2}-x_{3}^{2}
\end{equation}
The passages are essentially identical to those for the sphere, and the resulting metric is
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = \diff{\vec{x}^{\,2}} - \frac{(\vec{x}\cdot\diff{\vec{x}})^{2}}{a^{2}+\vec{x}^{\,2}}
\end{equation}
For our future discussions it will be really useful to define a scaled-down $x$ vector $\tilde{x} = \vec{x}/a$, so that the expression for the line element can be conveniently expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = a^{2}\left(\diff{\tilde{x}^{\,2}} + k \frac{(\tilde{x}\cdot\diff{\tilde{x}})^{2}}{1-k\tilde{x}^{2}}\right)
    \label{eq:usefulcompact}
\end{equation}
where we've introduced the parameter $k = 0, \pm 1$, which defines the topological properties of the space we're considering.
\par Sometimes (read: almost always) it is useful to express the metric through the polar coordinates of the 2-dimensional sphere. With such purpose in mind, we're defining $x_{3}$ as the "height" of a certain circular section, $r'$ the radius of such section, and $\theta$ the azimuthal angle with respect to axis parallel to $x_{1}$. In formulas
\begin{align*}
    x_{3}^{2} &= a^{2}-r'^{2}\\
    x_{1} &= r'\cos\theta\\
    x_{2} &= r'\sin\theta
\end{align*}
With this new set of coordinates, the line element of the 2-dimensional sphere becomes
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = \diff{r'^{2}} + r'^{2}\diff{\theta^{2}} + \frac{(r'\diff{r'})^{2}}{a^{2}-r'^{2}} = a^{2}\left(\frac{\diff{r^{2}}}{1-r^{2}} + r^{2}\diff{\theta^{2}}\right)
\end{equation}
where we've also introduced the adimensional radius $r = r'/a$, which lives in the interval $r \in [0,1]$. We can repeat the calculations for the hyperbola and the flat space in a similar fashion. The final result for the three scenarios we've considered can be once again expressed in a single expression thanks to the $k$ parameter
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = a^{2}\left(\frac{\diff{r}^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}+r^{2}\diff{\theta^{2}}\right)
\end{equation}

\subsubsection*{4-Dimensional Space}

Perhaps it might have occurred to you that we do not, in fact, live in a 3-dimensional space, but rather in a 4-dimensional one with 3-dimensional spatial sections. The purpose of this section is essentially to replicate the calculations we've already went through for the 3-dimensional space but with one more additional dimension to address.
\par For this purpose, it comes somewhat in handy recalling (\ref{eq:usefulcompact}), which allows us to immediately find the expression for the line element induced on a 3-dimensional surface embedded in a 4-dimensional space. All we have to do is replacing the metric of the 1-sphere $S_{1}$ ($\diff{\theta^{2}}$), with that of the 2-sphere $S_{2}$, the solid angle $\diff{\Omega_{(2)}^{2}}$
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = a^{2}\left(\diff{\tilde{x}^{\,2}} + k \frac{(\tilde{x}\cdot\diff{\tilde{x}})^{2}}{1-k\tilde{x}^{2}}\right) = a^{2}\left(\frac{\diff{r}^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}+r^{2}\diff{\Omega_{(2)}^{2}}\right)
\end{equation}
\par For example, the metric induced on the sphere ($k=1$) can be obtained defining the spherical coordinates $(\chi, \theta, \phi)$ and a radial distance $a$, so that
\begin{equation}
    x_{1}^{2} + x_{2}^{2}+x_{3}^{2}+x_{4}^{2} = a^{2}
\end{equation}
and the line element is 
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = \diff{x_{1}^{2}}+\diff{x_{2}^{2}}+\diff{x_{3}^{2}} +\diff{x_{4}^{2}}
\end{equation}
Performing the due substitutions you'd obtain
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = a^{2}(\diff{\chi^{2}}+\sin^{2}\chi\diff{\Omega_{(2)}^{2}})
\end{equation}
The metric induced on the hyperboloid is obtained replacing $\sin\chi$ with $\sinh\chi$. Note that the new radial coordinate $\chi$ is adimensional, is in the range $[0,\pi]$, and is defined through
\begin{equation}
    \diff{\chi} = \frac{\diff{r}}{\sqrt{1-kr^{2}}}
\end{equation}
\par In the hyperboloid case, the radial coordinate $\chi$ will now be in the range $[0, \infty)$, and the associated Cartesian coordinates are
\begin{align*}
    x_{1} &= a\sinh\chi\sin\theta\cos\phi\\
    x_{2} &= a\sinh\chi\sin\theta\sin\phi\\
    x_{3} &= a\sin\chi\cos\theta\\
    x_{4} &= a\cos\chi
\end{align*}
We can go through several boring substitutions to find the induced line element
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = a^{2}(\diff{\chi^{2}} + \sinh^{2}\chi\diff{\Omega_{(2)}^{2}})
\end{equation}
and the compact expression for the three surfaces we're considering is
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
        \diff{l^{2}} = a^{2}\left(\diff{\chi^{2}} + \frac{1}{k}\sin^{2}(\sqrt{k}\chi)\diff{\Omega_{(2)}^{2}}\right) \quad k=0,\pm 1
    }
\end{equation}
where we've made use of the fact that the line element can be conventiently expressed as
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = a^{2}(\diff{\chi^{2}} + f_{k}^{2}\diff{\Omega_{(2)}^{2}})
\end{equation}
with $f_{k}$ given by
\begin{equation}
    f_{k} = \left\{
    \begin{array}{ll}
        k^{-1/2}\sin(\sqrt{k}\chi) & k = 1\\
        \chi & k=0 \\
        (-k)^{-1/2}\sinh(\sqrt{-k}\chi) & k = -1
    \end{array}
    \right.
\end{equation}
which can be further compressed into
\begin{equation}
    f_{k} = (k)^{-1/2}\sin(\sqrt{k}\chi)
\end{equation}
which exploits the known limit for $k=0$ for the sine and the definition of the hyperbolic sine for $k=-1$.

\section{Friedmann-Robertson-Walker Metrics}

\par At this point we have all the elements needed to reintroduce time back into the picture
\begin{equation}
    \diff{s^{2}} = -\diff{t^{2}}+a^{2}(t)\tilde{g}_{ij}\diff{x^{i}}\diff{x^{j}} = -\diff{t^{2}}+\diff{l^{2}}
\end{equation}
\par If the Universe is spatially homogeneous and isotropic at all times, and if distances are allowed to expand or contract as a function of time only\footnote{This is an inevitable consequence of the request of having a metric that is isotropic and homogeneous, for if all points didn't evolve at the same rate, it would be impossible for the Universe to be isotropic.}, there's only one metric possible and is the FLRW metric:
\begin{equation}
    \text{d}s^{2} = -\text{d}t^{2}+a(t)^{2}\left(\frac{\text{d}r^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}+r^{2}\text{d}\Omega_{(2)}^{2}\right)
    \label{eq:FRW}
\end{equation}
Since $\diff{s^{2}} = g_{\mu\nu}\diff{x^{\mu}}\diff{x^{\nu}}$, the elements of the metric tensor can be read directly from (\ref{eq:FRW})
\begin{align*}
    g_{00} &= -1\\
    g_{rr} &= \frac{a^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}\\
    g_{\theta\theta} &= r^{2}a^{2}\\
    g_{\phi\phi} &= a^{2}r^{2}\sin^{2}\theta
\end{align*}
while $g_{0i}$ and off-diagonal terms of $g_{ij}$ are vanishing.
\par From the coefficient of the metric, we can also compute Christoffel's symbols
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma_{\mu\;\,\nu}^{\;\alpha} = \frac{1}{2}g^{\lambda\alpha}(\partial_{\mu}g_{\lambda\nu}+\partial_{\nu}g_{\mu\lambda}-\partial_{\lambda}g_{\mu\nu})
    \label{eq:christoffel}
\end{equation}
which have been reported in Appendix C.
\par Sometimes it is useful to perform a slight manipulation of the FLRW metric (\ref{eq:FRW}) and define a conformal time 
\begin{equation}
    a(\eta)\text{d}\eta = \text{d}t
    \label{eq:conformaltime}
\end{equation}
so that the metric is, guess what, conformal.
\begin{tcolorbox}[enhanced,attach boxed title to top center={yshift=-3mm,yshifttext=-1mm},
  colback=blue!5!white,colframe=blue!75!black,colbacktitle=blue!80!black,
  title=Conformal Metric,
  boxed title style={size=small,colframe=blue!50!black}]
  Two metrics are conformal if there exists a conformal transformation such that
  $$ \tilde{g}_{\mu\nu} = \Omega(\eta)g_{\mu\nu} $$
  and the metric $g_{\mu\nu}$ has no explicit dependence on the $\eta$ parameter.
\end{tcolorbox}
\par The scale factor $a$ is therefore factorized and (\ref{eq:FRW}) becomes
\begin{equation}
    \text{d}s^{2} = a(\eta)^{2}\left(-\text{d}\eta^{2}+\frac{\text{d}r^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}+r^{2}\text{d}\Omega_{(2)}^{2}\right)
    \label{eq:FRW_conf}
\end{equation}
Note that the quantity within parentheses is independent of the conformal time. On top of that, if $k=0$ (flat space), the FLRW metric is conformal to a Minkowski metric, and the metric is said "conformally flat".

\subsection{Geodesics}

Consider a particle initially at rest in a FLRW metric (\ref{eq:FRW}). The geodesics equation for such a particle will be
\begin{equation}
    \dder{x^{i}}{\tau} = -\Gamma_{0\;\,0}^{\;i}\der{x^{0}}{\tau}\der{x^{0}}{\tau}
\end{equation}
since $\text{d}x^{i}/\text{d}\tau = 0$, where $\tau$ is the particle's proper time. However, since $\Gamma_{0\;\,0}^{\;i} = 0$, the particle isn't accelerated and its radial coordinate remains constant!
\par Note that what remains constant is actually the particles \emph{comoving coordinate}, while its \emph{proper distance} calculated from some origin of the coordinate system changes because of the time-evolution of the scale factor $a$.
\par Once a system of axis is fixed so that a given point $P_{1} = (r_{1},0,0)$, we can define its proper distance as
\begin{equation}
    d(r,t) = \int_{0}^{r_{1}} \diff{l(r)}
\end{equation}
where $\diff{l(r)}$ is the line element induced on the manifold, in our case
\begin{equation}
    \diff{l^{2}} = a(t)^{2}\left(\frac{\text{d}r^{2}}{1-kr^{2}}+r^{2}\text{d}\Omega_{(2)}^{2}\right)
\end{equation}
Ignoring the angular dependence, i.e. a point object for which $\text{d}\Omega = 0$, the proper distance is
\begin{equation}
    d(r,t) = \int_{0}^{r_{1}} \frac{a(t)\diff{r}}{(1-kr^{2})^{1/2}}
\end{equation}
which is solved by 
\begin{equation}
    d(r,t) = a(t)\left\{
    \begin{array}{ll}
        \arcsin(r_{1}), & k=1\\
        r_{1}, & k=0\\
        \text{arcsinh}(r_{1}), & k=-1
    \end{array}
    \right.
\end{equation}
Please note that unlike it's done by other textbooks, like \cite{carroll}, here the scale factor $a$ has the dimension of a length rather than being adimensional. You can think of it as the result of the product between an adimensional scale factor (which is the one really changing) and the curvature radius of the Universe at some time.
\par Let us now consider a free particle with non-vanishing initial momentum. The general geodesics equation is
\begin{equation}
    \dder{x^{\sigma}}{\tau} = -\Gamma_{\mu\;\,\nu}^{\;\sigma}\der{x^{\mu}}{\tau}\der{x^{\nu}}{\tau}
    \label{eq:geodesicsequation}
\end{equation}
It's useful to remember the definition of the 4-momentum $p^{\mu} = mu^{\mu} = m\text{d}x^{\mu}/\text{d}\tau$ (valid for timelike paths, at least), from which we know that
\begin{equation*}
    |\vec{p}|^{2} = m^{2}g_{ij}\der{x^{i}}{\tau}\der{x^{j}}{\tau}
\end{equation*}
If we consider a system of coordinates such that at the instant considered the particles is arbitrarily close to the center of the coordinate system, the metric can thus be approximated as $\tilde{g}_{ij} = \delta_{ij} + \text{o}(x^{2})$, where we're essentially neglecting the scalar product contribution to (\ref{eq:usefulcompact}).
\par Then the Christoffel symbol $\Gamma_{j\;\,l}^{\;i} \propto \partial_{l}g^{i}_{\;j} \propto \text{o}(x)$ is negligible, since the space is always locally flat for each point we choose arbitrarily close to the origin.
\par The geodesics equation can be thus expanded 
\begin{align*}
    \dder{x^{i}}{\tau} = -\Gamma_{\mu\;\,\nu}^{\;i}\der{x^{\mu}}{\tau}\der{x^{\nu}}{\tau} &= -2\Gamma_{0\;\,j}^{\;i}\der{x^{j}}{\tau}\der{x^{0}}{\tau}\\
    & = -2\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}\delta^{i}_{\;j}\der{x^{j}}{\tau}\der{x^{0}}{\tau}
\end{align*}
At this point we can multiply both terms by $\derinline{\tau}{t}$
\begin{equation}
    \dder{x^{i}}{\tau}\cdot \der{\tau}{t} = -2\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}\delta^{i}_{\;j}\der{x^{j}}{\tau}\der{x^{0}}{\tau}\cdot \der{\tau}{t}
\end{equation}
and exploit the identity
\begin{equation}
    \der{x^{0}}{\tau}\cdot \der{\tau}{t} = 1
\end{equation}
resulting in a first-order differential equation for $\derinline{x^{i}}{\tau}$
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(\der{x^{i}}{\tau}\right) = -2\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}\der{x^{i}}{\tau}
\end{equation}
This equation yields as a solution $\derinline{x^{i}}{\tau} \propto a^{-2}$, which means that $|\vec{p}|^{2} \propto m^{2}a^{2}\cdot a^{-4} \propto a^{-2}$. This means that a massive particle "slows down" with respect to the comoving coordinates as the Universe expands!
\par If we instead consider a null geodesic (massless particle), we would find out that
\begin{equation}
    |\vec{p}| = p = \frac{\hbar}{\lambda} \implies \lambda \propto a(t)
\end{equation}
which means that the wavelength of a photon changes depending on the time at which it was emitted, resulting in an effect commonly known as \emph{cosmological redshift}.

\subsection{A short detour on redshift}

\par The purpose of this section is to show that we can obtain the same results that we've obtained in the last section using a more elegant approach that makes use of the symmetry properties of GR and the FLRW metric. Incidentally, this will also allow us to introduce a most useful quantity in Cosmology, which is the redshift $z$.
\par Consider the four-velocity of comoving observers $U^{\mu} = (1,0,0,0)$, then the metric admits a Killing tensor of the form 
\begin{equation}
    K_{\mu\nu} = a^{2}(g_{\mu\nu}+U_{\mu}U_{\nu})
    \label{eq:killingtensor}
\end{equation}
that you can check\footnote{Perhaps it was trivial, but it took me a long while to figure out and convince myself of the proof, which you'll find in Appendix D.} that it satisfies $\nabla_{(\sigma}K_{\mu\nu)} = 0$ where $\nabla_{\sigma}$ is the covariant derivative. By definition of Killing vector (the generalization to a K. tensor is straightforward), the quantity 
\begin{equation}
    K^{2} = K_{\mu\nu}V^{\mu}V^{\nu} = a^{2}\left[V_{\mu}V^{\mu}+(U_{\mu}V^{\mu})^{2}\right]
    \label{eq:conserved_killing}
\end{equation}
is conserved along the geodesics. Here $V^{\mu} = \text{d}x^{\mu}/\text{d}\lambda$. For massive particles, since $V_{\mu}V^{\mu} = -1$ (note the convention on the signature of the metric), we also have
\begin{equation*}
    (V^{0})^{2} = 1+|\vec{V}|^{2} \quad |\vec{V}|^{2} = g_{ij}V^{i}V^{j}
\end{equation*}
Recalling the formula for the four-velocity of the observer, we have $U_{\mu}V^{\mu} = -V^{0}$, so (\ref{eq:conserved_killing}) implies
\begin{equation*}
    |\vec{V}| = \frac{K}{a}
\end{equation*}
Notice how this means that the particle "slows down" with respect to the comoving coordinates as the Universe expands. If we instead consider null geodesics (that is what we're actually interested in)
\begin{equation*}
    V_{\mu}V^{\mu} = 0 \implies U_{\mu}V^{\mu} = \frac{K}{a}
\end{equation*}
but for null-like particles, like photons, we can write the four-velocity as $V^{\mu} = (\omega, \vec{k})$. So the frequency of the photon as measured by a comoving observer is $\omega = -U_{\mu}V^{\mu}$ (remember that $c=1$). The frequency emitted will still be given by the same functional expression but calculated in the local inertial frame of the emitter, so $\omega_{em} = -K/a_{em}$. We then find out that 
\begin{equation*}
    \frac{\omega_{obs}}{\omega_{em}} = \frac{a_{em}}{a_{obs}}
\end{equation*}
To make things easier we then define a quantity called \emph{redshift} defined as 
\begin{equation}
    z_{em} = \frac{\lambda_{obs}-\lambda_{em}}{\lambda_{em}}
    \label{eq:redshift_def}
\end{equation}
so that, if the observation takes place today ($a_{obs} = a_{0} = 1$), we can just write 
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    a_{em} = \frac{1}{1+z_{em}}
    }
    \label{eq:scalefactor_redshift}
\end{equation}
and 
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \frac{\nu_{obs}}{\nu_{em}} = \frac{1}{1+z_{em}}
    }
    \label{eq:cosmological_redshift}
\end{equation}
\par Let's give an example. If we observe, say, a galaxy with a redshift $z = 2$, we are observing it as it was when the Universe had a scale factor $a(t_{e}) = 1/3$.
\par The redshift we observe for a distant object thus depends only on the relative scale factors at the time of emission and the time of observation. It doesn't depend on how the transition between $a(t_{e})$ and $a(t_{0})$ was made: All that matters is the scale factors at the time of emission and the time of observation.
\par Calculating the redshift through spectroscopic observations, it was possible to correlate the recession velocity of objects in the Universe to their proper distance, obtaining the so called \emph{Hubble's law}
\begin{equation}
    v = cz = H_{0}d_{P}
    \label{eq:hubblelaw}
\end{equation}
Recent measurements \cite{Riess_2022} managed to estimate the present-day Hubble constant $H_{0}$ with great precision
\begin{equation}
    H_{0} = 72.53 \pm 0.99 \unit{km}\unit{s}^{-1}\unit{Mpc}^{-1}
    \label{eq:hubble_constant}
\end{equation}
from which we get an order-of-magnitude estimate of the age of the Universe
\begin{equation}
    H_{0}^{-1} = 9.78h^{-1}\unit{Gyr}
\end{equation}
using $H_{0} = 100h\unit{km}\unit{s}^{-1}\unit{Mpc}^{-1}$. We can also calculate the so-called Hubble radius
\begin{equation}
    R_{H} = H_{0}^{-1}c = 3h^{-1}\unit{Gpc}
\end{equation}
The variable $h$ keeps track of the dispersion on the value of $H_{0}$ produced by the different measurement techique that was implemented.
\par Please note that Hubble's law is only valid on relatively large scales, where the peculiar velocity of objects become much smaller than the expansion velocity at a given distance.
\par On a concluding note, please note that although it's a useful meter of comparison to understand what we're talking about, cosmological redshift has \emph{nothing to do} with a Doppler shift.
\par In fact, even if the Universe was in an age in which $\dot{a} = 0$ and so that $H_{0} = 0$, you wouldn't be able to interpret the shift in frequency as the effect of a Doppler shift ($v = 0$ after all), yet you'd still observe the shift, since $a(t) \neq 0$.

\subsection{Hubble-Le Maître's Law}

Let's get back to the definition of redshift starting from the scale factor $a$ which we can Taylor expand for emission times $t$ "close" to the observation time
\begin{equation}
    a(t) = a(t_{0}) + \left(\parder{a}{t}\right)_{t_{0}}(t-t_{0}) + \text{o}\left((t-t_{0})^{2}\right)
\end{equation}
so that
\begin{equation}
    a(t) = a(t_{0})\left[1 + H_{0}(t-t_{0}) + \text{o}\left((t-t_{0})^{2}\right)\right]
    \label{eq:a_expansion}
\end{equation}
The expansion won't be ill-defined only if $H_{0}^{-1} \gg (t-t_{0})$. We can now plug (\ref{eq:a_expansion}) into the definition of redshift (\ref{eq:scalefactor_redshift})
\begin{equation}
    z \approx H_{0}(t_{0}-t) \implies z = \frac{H_{0}d}{c}
    \label{eq:hubblelemaitre}
\end{equation}
where $d = c(t_{0}-t)$ is the distance spanned by photons in the same time interval. Equation (\ref{eq:hubblelemaitre}) is the correct way to express Hubble-Le Maître's law for not too large distances. For larger distances we would have to take into account the modification brought about by the second order term in the expansion of $a$, forcing us to introduce an additional parameter, the \emph{acceleration parameter} $q$.

\section{The road to Friedmann's Equations}

\subsection*{Conserved current for a non-collisional fluid}

For the time being, let's consider a Universe completely filled with a fluid of cosmological relevance with negligible collision rate. The number of particles that make the fluid is conserved, and since the fluid must obey the Cosmological Principle, collective motion of the fluid can't have a privileged direction, hence $\langle v^{i} \rangle = 0$. We can thus define a 4-current vector $J^{\mu}$ that we will shortly prove to be conserved \marginpar{Please note that we're walking, as usual, with units in which $c=1$.}
\begin{equation}
    J^{\mu} = nu^{\mu} = n\der{x^{\mu}}{\tau} \quad u^{\mu} = (u^{0}, \gamma\vec{v})
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number density of particles. To see how the expansion of the Universe affects the number density $n$ we have to first compute
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\mu}J^{\mu} = \partial_{\mu}J^{\mu} + \christoffel{\mu}{\nu}{\mu}J^{\nu}
\end{equation}
But since the metric is the FLRW, the covariant divergence of $J^{\mu}$ is vanishing. In fact, since $J^{i}$ is zero on average, the covariant divergence is reduced to simplu
\begin{equation}
    \partial_{0}J^{0} + \christoffel{i}{0}{i}J^{0} = 0 \quad \christoffel{i}{0}{i} = \frac{\dot{a}}{a}\delta^{i}_{\;\;i} = 3 \frac{\dot{a}}{a}
\end{equation}
which means
\begin{equation}
    \der{n}{t} = -3\frac{\dot{a}}{a} \implies n(t)\propto a^{-3}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Energy-Momentum Tensor and Its Conservation}

For the sake of simplicity, let's consider first the case of a flat-space. The energy-momentum tensor can be introduced from the minimal-action principle, imposing that the variation of a properly defined action functional is vanishing \emph{on shell}\footnote{i.e. when calculated on the equations of motion.}
\begin{equation}
    \delta S = \delta \left[\int \diff{t}\diff{V}\mathcal{L}\left(q^{(a)}, \parder{q^{(a)}}{x^{\mu}}\right)\right] = 0
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal{L}$ is a lagrangian density. Computing the variation we obtain Euler-Lagrange equations
\begin{equation}
    \partial^{\mu}\left(\parder{\mathcal{L}}{(\partial^{\mu}q)}\right) - \parder{\mathcal{L}}{q} = 0
    \label{eq:EL-eq}
\end{equation}
Exploiting Noether's theorem and assuming translational invariance for the lagrangian density we could show that there exists a conserved quantity representing the 4-momentum flux crossing the fixed time hypersurfaces, whose normal vector is tangent to the flux itself
\begin{equation}
    \partial_{\nu}\left[\parder{\mathcal{L}}{(\partial_{\nu}q)}\partial_{\mu}q - \delta^{\nu}_{\;\mu}\mathcal{L}\right] = \partial_{\nu}T^{\nu}_{\;\mu} = 0
\end{equation}
The 4-momentum is thus simply defined as the flux of the energy-momentum tensor through the hypersurfaces
\begin{equation}
    p^{\mu} = \int T^{\mu\nu}\diff{S_{\nu}} \quad \diff{S_{\nu}} = \diff{V}\hat{n}_{n}
\end{equation}
where $\hat{n}_{\nu} = (1,0,0,0)$ is the unit vector orthogonal to the fixed-time hypersurfaces. For reasons that I'm sure won't surprise you, $T^{00}$ is the energy density, while $T^{i0}$ is the momentum density. Moreover, you can easily check that 
\begin{equation}
    \int_{V} \partial_{\alpha}T^{i\alpha}\diff{V} = 0 \implies \partial_{t}\int_{V} T^{i0}\diff{V} + \int_{V} \partial_{j}T^{ij}\diff{V} = 0
\end{equation}
Applying the divergence theorem to the second integral yields
\begin{equation}
    \partial_{t}\int_{V} T^{i0}\diff{V} + \int_{\partial V} T^{ij}\diff{S_{j}} = 0
\end{equation}
which means that forces must be evenly balanced by the pressure arising in the fluid.
\par When a fluid is isotropic and homogeneous in a comoving reference frame, the fluid is said to be \emph{perfect}. Through long, and boring, calculations (which essentially consist in transforming $T^{00}$ and $T^{ij}$ out of the comoving frame), you can show that a perfectly valid expression for the energy-momentum tensor of a perfect fluid is \marginpar{Note that the components of $u^{\mu}$ are fixed by requiring $u_{\mu}u^{\mu} = -1$}
\begin{equation}
    T^{\mu\nu} = (p+\rho)u^{\mu}u^{\nu} + pg^{\mu\nu}
    \label{eq:energymomentumtensor-cov}
\end{equation}
which we can also present in the form of a (1,1) tensor
\begin{equation}
    T^{\mu}_{\;\nu} = g_{\alpha\nu}T^{\mu\alpha} = (p+\rho)u^{\mu}u_{\nu} + p(g^{\mu\alpha}g_{\nu\alpha})
\end{equation}
from which we can immediately compute the trace
\begin{equation}
    T^{\mu}_{\;\mu} = -(p+\rho) + 4p = 3p-\rho
\end{equation}
What we'd like to show now is that the energy-momentum tensor (\ref{eq:energymomentumtensor-cov}) we've defined is conserved $\nabla_{\nu}T^{\mu\nu} = 0$ also if the metric is no longer that of the flat space but rather the FLRW (\ref{eq:FRW}). To do so, we have to prove that
\begin{equation}
    \nabla_{\nu}T^{\mu\nu} = \nabla_{\nu}\left[(p+\rho)u^{\mu\nu}\right] + \nabla_{\nu}\left[pg^{\mu\nu}\right] = 0
\end{equation}
After exploiting metric compatibility, and expanding the covariant derivative, we can rewrite the previous expression as
\begin{equation}
    \partial_{\nu}(p+\rho)u^{\mu}u^{\nu} + \partial_{\nu}p g^{\mu\nu} + (p+\rho)\left[\christoffel{\nu}{\lambda}{\mu}u^{\lambda}u^{\nu}+\christoffel{\nu}{\lambda}{\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\lambda}\right]
\end{equation}
At this point we can notice that, since the fluid is comoving, $u^{\mu} = (1,0,0,0)$, and thus
\begin{equation}
    \christoffel{\nu}{\lambda}{\mu}u^{\lambda}u^{\nu} = \christoffel{0}{0}{\mu}u^{0}u^{0} \quad \christoffel{\nu}{\lambda}{\nu}u^{\mu}u^{\lambda} = \christoffel{\nu}{0}{\nu}u^{\mu}u^{0}
\end{equation}
But $\christoffel{0}{0}{\mu} = 0$ for all values of $\mu$ given the FLRW metric, while the second term can be simplified as
\begin{equation}
    \christoffel{\nu}{0}{\nu}u^{\mu}u^{0} = \frac{1}{2}g^{\nu i}\partial_{0}g_{\nu i}u^{\mu}
\end{equation}
\par Let's focus on the purely spatial component $\mu = j$, which ultimately simplifies to just $\partial_{i}p g^{ij}$, which is vanishing since the pressure has to be homogeneous because of the Cosmological Principle. The only non-trivial component is the temporal one $\mu = 0$, which yields
\begin{equation}
    \partial_{0}(p+\rho)-\partial_{0}p +\frac{p+\rho}{2}g^{ij}\partial_{0}g_{ij}
\end{equation}
The last derivative can be hand-wavingly calculated expanding $g_{ij} = a^{2}\tilde{g}_{ij}$, where $\tilde{g}_{ij}$ is independent of time. This way, the temporal component ultimately yields
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \partial_{0}\rho + 3(p+\rho)H(t) = 0
    }
    \label{eq:fluidequation}
\end{equation}
It might be worth pointing out that $\rho$ is here an energy density rather than a matter density.

\subsection{Ricci Tensor in a FLRW Metric}

Honestly, I'm not in the mood to calculate all non-trivial components of the Riemann tensor first
\begin{equation}
    R^{\mu}_{\;\nu\alpha\beta} = \partial_{\alpha}\christoffel{\nu}{\beta}{\alpha} - \partial_{\beta}\christoffel{\nu}{\mu}{\alpha} + \christoffel{\sigma}{\alpha}{\mu}\christoffel{\nu}{\beta}{\sigma} - \christoffel{\sigma}{\beta}{\mu}\christoffel{\nu}{\alpha}{\sigma}
    \label{eq:riemann tensor}
\end{equation}
to then calculate Ricci's $R_{\nu\beta} =  R^{\alpha}_{\;\nu\alpha\beta}$. You can find all non-trivial components of the Ricci tensor in Appendix C. Without doing all the troublesome calculations, I'll just say that in principle the calculation is made a little easier if we consider small deviations from flat-space for the time independent metric $\tilde{g}_{ij} = g_{ij}/a^{2}$, which helps significantly to decrease the effort.
\par It is worth mentioning, however, that we might as well expect the components $R_{0i}$ to be vanishing because of the request of homogeneity and isotropy. An elegant way of proving it without losing your wits in the process might be considering the "inverted" version of Einstein's equation
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G \left(T_{\mu\nu} -\frac{1}{2}Tg_{\mu\nu}\right)
    \label{eq:einstein_inverted}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the trace of the energy-momentum tensor. From here is trivial to prove our previous claim if we assume $T_{\mu\nu}$ to be the energy momentum tensor of a perfect fluid as seen by a comoving observer.
\par For future reference, I'm going to write the non-trivial components of the Ricci tensor down below
\begin{equation}
    R_{00} = -\frac{3\ddot{a}}{a} \quad R_{ij} = \frac{1}{a^{2}}(2k + a\ddot{a}+2\dot{a}^{2})g_{ij}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Friedmann's Equation}

Consider the non-canonical form of Einstein's equation (\ref{eq:einstein_inverted}). The trace of the energy momentum tensor is (\ref{eq:energymomentumtensor-cov}), and its trace is $T = 3p - \rho$, so it's immediate to solve Einstein's equation.
\par The temporal component yields
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
        \frac{\ddot{a}}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}(\rho + 3p)
    }
    \label{eq:friedmann1}
\end{equation}
\par Since we have three independent variables and only two viable equations\footnote{The equations are actually 3: Two Friedmann equations and the equation coming from the conservation of the temporal component of the energy-momentum tensor. The three of them are not independent, since they're related through Bianchi's identity.}, we're in the need of introducing and equation of state (EoS) that relates the pressure to the energy density. Cosmology usually deals with dilute gases, for which the equation of state is simple.
\par For substances of cosmological importance
\begin{equation}
    p = w\rho
    \label{eq:eos}
\end{equation}
where $w$ is a dimensionless number. The behaviors of our favorite sources are summarized in the following table
\begin{table}[h!]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c|c}
        & $w_{i}$ \\
        \hline
        matter & 0\\
        radiation & $\frac{1}{3}$\\
        curvature & $-\frac{1}{3}$\\
        vacuum & $-1$
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}
\par With our newly gained EoS in mind, we can rewrite Friedmann equation as
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\ddot{a}}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\rho (1+w)
\end{equation}
which tells us that the Universe is accelerating if and only if
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\ddot{a}}{a} > 0 \iff w < -\frac{1}{3}
\end{equation}
\par The spatial component of (\ref{eq:einstein_inverted}), on the other hand, yields
\begin{equation}
    \frac{2k}{a^{2}} + \frac{\ddot{a}}{a} +2\left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2} = -4\pi G(p-\rho)
\end{equation}
where the common factor $\tilde{g}_{ij}$ was suppressed. It is customary however to subtract the 1st Friedmann equation (\ref{eq:friedmann1}) from the equation we've just derived, obtaining the 2nd Friedmann equation
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2} = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho -\frac{k}{a^{2}}
    }
    \label{eq:friedmann2}
\end{equation}
\par Suppose that the Universe contains $N$ different components, with the $i$-th component having an energy density $\rho_{i}$ and an EoS parameter $w_{i}$. As long as there is no interaction between the different components, the fluid equation (\ref{eq:fluidequation}) must hold for each component separately. So
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\dot{\rho}_{i}}{\rho_{i}} = -3(1+w_{i})\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}
\end{equation}
which can be immediately integrated. Behold:
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \rho_{i}(a) = \rho_{i,0}\left(\frac{a(t)}{a(t_{0})}\right)^{-3(1+w_{i})}
    }
    \label{eq:density_scalefactor}
\end{equation}
Equation (\ref{eq:density_scalefactor}) implies that for (non-relativistic) matter $\rho \propto a^{-3}$, while for radiation (or relativistic matter) $\rho\propto a^{-4}$. This fact has a simple explanation: The energy density of non-relativistic matter decreases as $a$ increases because of the expanding volume of the Universe, while relativistic matter also gets redshifted. This has also another striking consequence: There had been times when different components were equivalent!
\begin{align*}
    & \frac{\rho_{\Lambda}(a)}{\rho_{m}(a)}=1=\frac{\rho_{\Lambda,0}}{\rho_{m,0}}a^{3} \implies a_{\Lambda,m} = \left(\frac{\rho_{m,0}}{\rho_{\Lambda,0}}\right)^{1/3} \approx 0.766 \implies z_{\Lambda,m}= 0.31\\
    & \frac{\rho_{m}(a)}{\rho_{r}(a)}=1=\frac{\rho_{m,0}}{\rho_{r,0}}a \; \implies a_{r,m} = \frac{\rho_{r,0}}{\rho_{m,0}} \approx 2.9\cdot 10^{-4} \implies z_{r,m}= 3400
\end{align*}
If the Universe contains different components with different values of $w$, in the limit $a\to 0$, the component with the largest value of $w$ is dominant.
\par Now that we have a relation between $\rho$ and $a$ we can set to solve the Friedmann equation (\ref{eq:friedmann2}) in special case of a spatially flat ($k = 0$) and single-component Universe.
Plugging in (\ref{eq:density_scalefactor}), we find 
\begin{equation}
    \dot{a}^{2} = \frac{8\pi G \rho_{0}}{c^{2}}\frac{a^{-(1+3w)}}{a_{0}^{-(1+3w)}}
\end{equation}
which can be solved explicitly \marginpar{Also note that $\dot{a} \propto (\rho a^{2})^{1/2}$ which is also proportional to $\propto a^{-(1+3w)/2}$.\\ Hence, a fluid with $w = -1/3$ would have constant expansion rate $\dot{a}$.}
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
        a(t) = a_{0}\left(\frac{t}{t_{0}}\right)^{\frac{2}{3+3w}} \quad t_{0} = \frac{2}{3H_{0}(1+w)} \quad w \neq -1
    }
    \label{eq:scalefactor_ev}
\end{equation}
For $w = -1$, the equation is solved by
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    a(t) = a_{0}e^{\pm H_{0}(t-t_{0})}
    }
    \label{eq:scalefactor_ev_cc}
\end{equation}

\subsubsection{A detour on conformal time}

\par Let's now write Friedmann's equations using the conformal time $\diff{\eta} = \diff{t}/a$. Through a basic application of the chain rule
\begin{equation}
    \der{a}{t} = \der{a}{\eta}\der{\eta}{t} = a' \frac{1}{a}
\end{equation}
where with the superscript we're indicating derivatives with respect to the conformal time. In short
\begin{equation}
    a' = \dot{a}a \quad a'' = a(a\ddot{a}+\dot{a}a') \quad \mathcal{H} = \frac{a'}{a} = \dot{a} \quad \mathcal{H}' = a\ddot{a}
\end{equation}
With these new variables, Friedmann's equation become
\begin{align}
    \label{eq:f2_conf}
    \mathcal{H}^{2} &= \frac{8\pi G}{3}a^{2}\rho - k\\
    \mathcal{H}' &= -\frac{4\pi G}{3}a^{2}(\rho + 3p)
    \label{eq:f1_conf}
\end{align}
while the conformal fluid equation is
\begin{equation}
    \rho' + 3\mathcal{H}(p+\rho) = 0
    \label{eq:fluideq_conf}
\end{equation}
\par Assuming $w \neq 1$, we can integrate the definition of conformal time (\ref{eq:conformaltime}) to obtain the scale factor as a function of the conformal time
\begin{equation}
    \eta - \eta_{0} = \int_{t_{0}}^{t} a^{-1}\diff{t} \propto \int_{t_{0}}^{t} t^{-2/3(1+w)}\diff{t}
\end{equation}
which yields
\begin{equation}
    a(\eta) \propto \eta^{2/1+3w} \quad (w \neq -1)
    \label{eq:scalefactor_ev_conf}
\end{equation}
\par If, on the other hand, $w = -1$, the integral is trivial and we find that
\begin{equation}
    a(\eta) \propto -\frac{1}{\eta - \eta_{0}} \quad (\eta - \eta_{0} < 0)
    \label{eq:scalefactor_ev_conf_cc}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Einstein Universe}

Consider a stationary Universe for which Einstein's equation is modified to include the addition of Cosmological Constant $\Lambda$
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}Rg_{\mu\nu} + \Lambda g_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G T_{\mu\nu}
\end{equation}
and consider the modified energy-momentum tensor $\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu}$ defined so to include the contribution of the stray Cosmological Constant
\begin{equation}
    R_{\mu\nu} - \frac{1}{2}Rg_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G \tilde{T}_{\mu\nu} = 8\pi G\left(T_{\mu\nu}-\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G}g_{\mu\nu}\right)
\end{equation}
Moreover we can compute the new energy density
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{T}_{00} = \rho + \frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G}
\end{equation}
and define an energy density for vacuum
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{\Lambda} =  \frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G}
    \label{eq:vacuum_ed}
\end{equation}
Similarly, we can also calculate the new pressure exerted by the cosmological fluid
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{p} = \tilde{T}_{ii} = \left(p - \frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G}\right)g_{ii} = p - \frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G}
\end{equation}
and define the pressure for vacuum, which turns out to be 
\begin{equation}
    p_{\Lambda} =  -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G} = -\rho_{\Lambda}
    \label{eq:vacuum_p}
\end{equation}
So the vacuum, or Cosmological Constant, would behave like a fluid with $w = -1$. We can see what that implies if we plug this last result into Freedman's equations
\begin{align*}
    3\ddot{a} &= -4\pi Ga(\tilde{\rho}+3\tilde{p})\\
    a\ddot{a} + 2\dot{a}^{2} + 2k &= 4\pi G a^{2}(\tilde{\rho}-\tilde{p})
\end{align*}
which under Einstein's assumption of a static Universe ($\dot{a} = 0$ and $ \ddot{a} = 0$) imply 
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\rho} = -3\tilde{p} \quad 2k = 4\pi G a^{2}(\tilde{\rho}-\tilde{p})
\end{equation}
and so
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\rho} = \frac{3k}{8\pi G a^{2}} \quad \tilde{p} = -\frac{k}{8\pi G a^{2}}
\end{equation}
The conditions for a static Universe thus require it to be a \emph{closed Universe} ($k=1$). If we assume non-relativistic matter to be the only component of $T_{\mu\nu}$, then
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{p} = -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G} \implies  \tilde{p} = -\frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G} = -\frac{k}{8\pi G a^{2}}
\end{equation}
and thus
\begin{equation}
    \Lambda = \frac{k}{a^{2}}
\end{equation}
This means that the physical dimension of the Universe would be fixed and equal to $a = \Lambda^{-1/2}$ ($k=1$). This already conflicts with present observations (since $H_{0} \neq 0$), but to push worse to worst, Einstein's Universe would also be unstable.
\par Consider in fact $\tilde{T}_{\mu\nu} = T_{\mu\nu} + T^{\Lambda}_{\mu\nu}$, so that \marginpar{You have to simply substitute (\ref{eq:vacuum_ed}) and the value we've found for $\tilde{\rho}$, using $\Lambda = a^{-1}$.}
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{\rho} = \rho_{M} + \rho_{\Lambda} \implies \rho_{M} = \frac{2\Lambda}{8\pi G}
\end{equation}
If we add a positive perturbation the scale factor $a = a_{E} + \Delta a$, then the matter energy density decreases
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{M} = \frac{3k}{8\pi G a^{2}} - \frac{\Lambda}{8\pi G}
\end{equation}
so that we could write in principle $\rho_{M} \to \rho_{M} + \Delta\rho_{M}$ with $\Delta\rho_{M} < 0$. But if we plug this into Friedmann's equation
\begin{equation}
    \ddot{a} = -4\pi G a (\tilde{\rho}_{0}+3\tilde{p}_{0} + \Delta\rho_{M}) = - 4\pi G a \Delta\rho_{M} = 4\pi G a |\Delta\rho_{M}| > 0
\end{equation}
meaning that such a Universe is unstable!

\subsubsection{Einstein-de Sitter Universe}

Let's try this one more time. Recall Friedmann's second equation (\ref{eq:friedmann2}) and consider a flat Universe with only the Cosmological Constant in it
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2} = \frac{\Lambda}{3} \implies a(t) \propto \exp\left(\sqrt{\frac{\Lambda}{3}}t\right)
\end{equation}
and so Hubble's constant $H = \sqrt{\Lambda/3}$. This implies 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\ddot{a}}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}(\rho_{\Lambda}+3p_{\Lambda}) = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho_{\Lambda} = \frac{\Lambda}{3}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Critical Density}

Let's write again Friendmann's second equation
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{\dot{a}}{a}\right)^{2} = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho -\frac{k}{a^{2}}
\end{equation}
and define the \emph{critical energy density} as the energy density the Universe should have to be flat \marginpar{At present time, the critical density $\rho_{C}(t_{0})$ is evaluated at $ 1.878\power{10}{-29}h^{2}\unit{g}\unit{cm}^{-3}$ or, equivalently, at $1.05\power{10}{4} h^{2}\unit{eV}\unit{cm}^{-3}$.}
\begin{equation}
    \rho_{C} = \frac{3H^{2}}{8\pi G}
    \label{eq:criticaldensity}
\end{equation}
\par This quantity, that will generally change with time, is called the critical density because the Friedmann equation (\ref{eq:friedmann2}) can be written as
\begin{equation}
    \Omega - 1 = \frac{k}{H^{2}a^{2}}
    \label{eq:densityparam_eq}
\end{equation}
that univocally determinates the sign of $k$ depending on whether $\Omega$ is greater then, equal to, or less than one.
\begin{align*}
    & \rho < \rho_{c} \iff \Omega < 1 \iff k < 0 \iff \text{open} \\
    & \rho = \rho_{c} \iff \Omega = 1 \iff k = 0 \iff \text{flat} \\
    & \rho > \rho_{c} \iff \Omega > 1 \iff k > 0 \iff \text{closed} 
\end{align*}
The density parameter $\Omega$, then, tells us which of the three FLRW geometries describes our Universe. Since the right-hand side of (\ref{eq:densityparam_eq}) cannot change sign as the Universe expands, neither can the left-hand side. So, $\Omega$ will have the same sign \emph{at all times}.
\par In a realistic case, we should consider a Universe with multiple components, although perhaps not interacting. We can then think of writing a total energy density
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \sum_{i}\rho_{i} = \rho_{DM} + \rho_{B} + \rho_{r} + \rho_{\Lambda} + ... 
    \label{eq:totaldensity}
\end{equation}
or, in terms of the density parameter(s), we could also write 
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \rho_{C}\sum_{i}\Omega_{i}
\end{equation}
\par In these variables, the Friedmann equation (\ref{eq:friedmann2}) can be written
\begin{equation}
    H^{2} = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\sum_{i(c)}\rho_{i}
    \label{eq:friedmann_alt}
\end{equation}
where the notation $\sum_{i(c)} = \Omega_{0} + \sum_{i}\Omega_{i}$ indicates that we sum not only over all the actual components of energy density $\rho_{i}$, but also on the contribution of spatial curvature, which we define as
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{0} = -\frac{k}{a^{2}H^{2}}
    \label{eq:spatial_dp}
\end{equation}
\par Dividing both sides by $H^{2}$ yields a shockingly simple equation
\begin{equation}
    1 = \sum_{i(c)}\Omega_{i} = \Omega_{0} + \sum_{i}\Omega_{i}
\end{equation}
thus producing a consistency relation that must hold at \emph{any} time.

\subsubsection{Acceleration Parameter}

A few pages ago, back when we first proved Hubble-Le Maître's law, we've introduced, although barely, the \emph{acceleration parameter} $q$, which we now define as 
\begin{equation}
    q = -\frac{\ddot{a}a}{\dot{a}^{2}}
    \label{eq:decelerationparam}
\end{equation}
I don't think I will surprise any of you saying that if $q < 0$, then the Universe is accelerating, and if it's $q > 0$, then it is decelerating. Not being surprised was what people back then expected when they first defined this quantity. They were so sure of their results that they even named it \emph{deceleration parameter} rather than acceleration. Shortly after, they realized they should have probably changed its name.
\par Recall (\ref{eq:friedmann1}) with a perfect fluid EoS
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\ddot{a}}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3} \rho(1+3w)
\end{equation}
This means that the "deceleration" parameter can be written as 
\begin{equation}
    q = \frac{4\pi G}{3 H^{2}} \rho (1+3w)
\end{equation}
or, equivalently, we can plug in the critical density and density parameter to find 
\begin{equation}
    q = \frac{\Omega}{2}(1+3w) \implies q = \sum_{i}\frac{\Omega_{i}}{2}(1+3w_{i})
\end{equation}
which can be studied for all the various components our Universe could feature
\begin{itemize}
    \item $w < -1/3 \implies q < 0$ and hence the Universe accelerates;
    \item $w=0 \implies q = \Omega_{M}/2 > 0$ and hence it decelerates. This was actually the underlying assumption in the Einstein-de Sitter Universe model;
    \item $w = 1/3 \implies q \approx \Omega_{r} >0$;
    \item $w = -1 \implies q = -\Omega_{\Lambda} < 0$ and hence the Universe accelerates. This is what a de Sitter Universe would predict.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dynamic Age of the Universe}

The actual model for the Universe, sometimes known as Benchmark model, is a $\Lambda$CDM model, where the $\Lambda$ obviously stands for Cosmological Constant, while CDM stands for \emph{Cold Dark Matter}.
\par We've already seen that we can write the total energy density as
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \rho_{m} + \rho_{r} + \rho_{\Lambda}
\end{equation}
which all scale differently but according to (\ref{eq:density_scalefactor}). This is most useful, because it allows us to write the total energy density as a function of the present-day density parameters and their associates scaling with the scale factor
\begin{equation}
    \rho = \frac{3H_{0}^{2}}{8\pi G}\left(\Omega_{\Lambda,0} + \Omega_{r,0}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{4} + \Omega_{m,0}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{3}\right)
\end{equation}
We can plug this into (\ref{eq:friedmann2}) to obtain
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\dot{a}^{2}}{a_{0}^{2}H_{0}^{2}} + \frac{k}{a_{0}^{2}H_{0}^{2}} = \frac{8\pi G}{3H_{0}^{2}}\left(\frac{a}{a_{0}}\right)^{2}\rho
\end{equation}
It might be useful to define and introduce the parameter $x = a(t)/a_{0} = 1/(1+z(t))$ which lives in the range $0 \leq x \leq 1$ since $a(t) \leq a_{0}$ (we're only looking back). With a realistic model, such as the Benchmark, we can safely put $k=0$ and simplify the equation
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\dot{a}^{2}}{a_{0}^{2}}\frac{1}{H_{0}^{2}} = \left(\frac{a}{a_{0}}\right)^{2}\left(\Omega_{\Lambda,0} + \Omega_{r,0}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{4} + \Omega_{m,0}\left(\frac{a_{0}}{a}\right)^{3}\right)
\end{equation}
which we can rewrite as 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{H^{2}}{H_{0}^{2}}\cdot x^{2} = x^{2}\left[\Omega_{\Lambda,0}+\Omega_{m,0}x^{-3} + \Omega_{r,0}x^{-4}\right] = x^{2}E(x)^{2}
\end{equation}
and further manipulate
\begin{equation}
    \frac{H}{H_{0}}x = xE(x) \to \frac{1}{a}\dot{a}\frac{1}{H_{0}}x = xE(x) \to \frac{1}{a_{0}}\der{a}{t}\frac{1}{H_{0}} = xE(x)
\end{equation}
But since, by virtue of its own definition, we have 
\begin{equation}
    \diff{x} = \frac{\diff{a}}{a_{0}} \to \diff{t} = \frac{\diff{x}}{xH_{0}E(x)}
\end{equation}
This we can easily integrate from $x=0$ ($t=0$, which corresponds to the BBN in the standard model of the Hot Big Bang)
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    t(x) = \int_{0}^{x}\diff{t} = \int_{0}^{x} \frac{\diff{x'}}{x'H_{0}E(x')}
    }
    \label{eq:dynamicalage}
\end{equation}
which represents the \emph{dynamical age of the Universe}. Sometimes it might be useful to express it in terms of the redshift $z$ so that
\begin{equation}
    E^{2}(z) = \Omega_{\Lambda,0} + \Omega_{m,0}(1+z)^{3} + \Omega_{r,0}(1+z)^{4} \quad \diff{x} = -\frac{\diff{z}}{(1+z)^{2}}
\end{equation}
\par The dynamical age in terms of the redshift is then
\begin{equation}
    t(z) = \frac{1}{H_{0}}\int_{z}^{\infty} \frac{\diff{z'}}{(1+z')E(z')}
\end{equation}
\par For the Einstein-de Sitter Universe ($k=0$, $\Omega_{m,0}=1$), the dynamical time evaluates to 
\begin{equation}
    t_{0}^{E-dS} = \frac{1}{H_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\diff{x}}{x\cdot x^{-3/2}} = \frac{2}{3H_{0}} \approx 7.0h^{-1}\unit{Gyr}
\end{equation}
which is smaller than a Hubble time $H_{0}^{-1}$. The dynamical time produced by a E-dS Universe is way too short: It's in tension with the estimated age of some structures and/or unstable chemical elements which we can still observe (e.g., radionuclids with decay time of about $10-15\unit{Gyr}$). It is also in high tension with the estimated age of some old white dwarves and with the estimated age we've obtained from the temperature fluctuations of the CMB.
\par For a realistic $\Lambda$CDM model ($k=0$, $\Omega_{r,0}\sim 0$, and $\Omega_{\Lambda,0}+\Omega_{m,0}\sim 1$), the dynamical time is evaluated to
\begin{align*}
    t_{0} &\approx \frac{1}{H_{0}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\diff{x}}{x(\Omega_{\Lambda,0}+\Omega_{m,0}x^{-3})^{1/2}}\\
    &= \frac{1}{H_{0}\Omega_{\Lambda,0}^{1/2}}\int_{0}^{1}\frac{\diff{x}}{x(1+\left(\frac{1}{\Omega_{\Lambda,0}}-1\right)x^{-3})^{1/2}}
\end{align*}
which yields
\begin{equation}
    t_{0} = \frac{2}{3H_{0}}\frac{1}{\Omega_{\Lambda,0}^{1/2}}\ln\left[\frac{1+\Omega_{\Lambda,0}^{1/2}}{(1-\Omega_{\Lambda,0})^{1/2}}\right] \sim 14\unit{Gyr}
\end{equation}

\section{Inflation}

\subsection{Luminosity Distance}

If the redshift is not very small, we have to think more carefully about what we mean by "distance" in cosmology. The instantaneous physical distance (proper distance) is a convenient construct, but not itself observable, since observation always refer to our past light cone. In Euclidean space, there are a number of different ways to infer the distance of an object, for example comparing its apparent brightness to its intrinsic luminosity.
\par In a Euclidean space, we could define the \emph{luminosity distance} as
\begin{equation*}
    d_{L} = \left(\frac{L}{4\pi F}\right)^{1/2}
\end{equation*}
with intrisic luminosity $L$ and $F$. But how does this change in a curved and expanding Universe, and why?
\par For two reasons mainly:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Geometry}: The space is no longer Euclidean, and the surface of the 2-sphere onto which the luminosity is diluted is $4\pi a_{0}^{2}\tilde{r}_{s}^{2} = 4\pi a_{0}^{2}f_{k}(\chi)^{2}$, where $f_{k}(\chi)$ is 
    \begin{equation}
        \chi = \int_{0}^{r_{s}}\frac{\diff{r}}{\sqrt{1-kr^{2}}} = f_{k}^{-1}(r_{s}) = \left\{
            \begin{array}{ll}
                r_{s} & k=0\\
                \arcsin(r_{s}) & k=1\\
                \text{arcsinh}(r_{s}) & k=-1
            \end{array}
        \right.
    \end{equation}
    \item \emph{Correction to the concept of luminosity}: In an expanding Universe, we have to be a little more cautious, and the necessary steps to be taken are explained in detail in the following.
\end{enumerate}
\par Because of the expansion the wavelength of these photons will change according to (\ref{eq:redshift_def}). Therefore, the energy of a photon will decrease
\begin{equation*}
    E_{0} \sim \frac{c}{\lambda_{0}} = \frac{c}{(1+z)\lambda_{e}} \sim \frac{E_{e}}{1+z}
\end{equation*}
\par On top of that, two wavefronts separated by $\delta t_{e}$ will arrive with a delay $\delta t_{0} = \delta t_{e}(1+z)$, so the arrival rate will decrease of another $1+z$
\begin{equation*}
    L_{e} \sim \frac{E_{e}}{\delta t_{e}} = \frac{(1+z)^{2}E_{0}}{\delta t_{0}} = (1+z)^{2}L_{0}
\end{equation*}
\par Since the flux is equal to energy per unit time per unit area, we can write
\begin{equation*}
    F = \frac{L_{e}}{4\pi a_{0}^{2}\tilde{r}_{s}^{2}(1+z)^{2}} = \frac{L_{e}}{4\pi a_{0}^{2}f_{k}(\chi)^{2}(1+z)^{2}}
\end{equation*}
so that by direct comparison with out initial equation, we find that the \emph{luminosity distance} is
\begin{equation}
    d_{L} = a_{0}\tilde{r}_{s}(1+z) = (1+z)a_{0}f_{k}(\chi)
    \label{eq:luminositydistance}
\end{equation}
\par The luminosity distance is something we might hope to measure, since there are some astrophysical sources whose absolute luminosity are known, but $\chi$ (and, for extension, the comoving distance $\tilde{r}_{s}$), is not directly observable, so we have to remove it from our equation. On a null radial geodesic (for convenience), we have
\begin{equation*}
    0 = \text{d}s^{2} = -\text{d}t^{2} + a^{2}\text{d}\chi^{2}
\end{equation*}
or more simply 
\begin{equation*}
    \chi = \int \frac{\text{d}t}{a} = \int \frac{\text{d}a}{a^{2}H(a)}
\end{equation*}
\par It is conventional to convert the scale factor to redshift using (\ref{eq:scalefactor_redshift}) $a = (1+z)^{-1}$, so we have
\begin{equation*}
    \chi(z) = \int_{0}^{z}\frac{\text{d}z'}{H(z')}
\end{equation*}
\par In order to evaluate the Hubble parameter in this integral we can use Friedmann equation (\ref{eq:friedmann_alt}), thus finding 
\begin{equation*}
    d_{L}(z) = (1+z)a_{0}f_{k}\left[H_{0}^{-1}\int_{0}^{z} \frac{\text{d}z'}{E(z')}\right]
\end{equation*}
where we've defined $E(z) = H(z)/H_{0}$. Still, it is more common to speak in terms of $\Omega_{c,0} = -k/H_{0}^{2}$, which can be measured. We can therefore write the luminosity in terms of measurable cosmological parameters as 
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    d_{L}(z) = (1+z)\frac{H_{0}^{-1}}{\sqrt{|\Omega_{c,0}|}}f_{k}\left[\sqrt{|\Omega_{c,0}|}\int \frac{\text{d}z'}{E(z')}\right]
    }
    \label{eq:general_lumdist}
\end{equation}
\par Although it appears unwieldy (and it is), this equation is of central importance in cosmology. Given the observables $H_{0}$ and $\Omega_{i,0}$, we can straightforwardly calculate the luminosity distance to an object at any redshift $z$.
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/lumdist.png}
    \caption{Luminosity distance of a standard candle with observed redshift $z$, in units of the Hubble distance, $c/H_{0}$. The bold solid line gives the result for the Benchmark Model. For comparison, the dot-dash line indicates a flat, $\Lambda$-dominated Universe, and the dotted line a flat, matter-dominated Universe.}
    \label{fgr:lumdist}
\end{figure}
\par Experimentally, it's more common to use a quantity known as \emph{distance module} to quantify distances. It is related to the luminosity distance through the definition of absolute magnitude
\begin{equation}
    \mu = 5\log_{10}\left(\frac{d_{L}}{10\unit{pc}}\right)
    \label{eq:distancemodule}
\end{equation}
Remarkably, going back in time (so at higher redshift) we observe a change in the slope of the luminosity distance curve (or, equivalently, of the distance module) at roughly $z = 0.5$ caused by the change from an accelerating Universe ($\Lambda$DU) to a decelerating Universe (MDU).
\par If we define $z_{\Lambda}$ the redshift at which there is equivalence between the $\Lambda$ and matter density parameters 
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{\Lambda,0} = \Omega_{m,0}(1+z_{\Lambda})^{3} \implies z_{\Lambda} = \left(\frac{\Omega_{\Lambda,0}}{\Omega_{m,0}}\right)^{1/3}-1
\end{equation}
we find it to be $z_{\Lambda}\approx 0.44$, which is the exact cause of the \href{https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_coincidence_problem}{\emph{cosmic coincidence problem}}, since it appears rather odd that the Cosmological Constant is dominating at a redshift so close to present time.

\subsection{Second Order Hubble Law}

If you recall, earlier we've defined Hubble-Le Maître's law (\ref{eq:hubblelaw}) at the linear order. It is the purpose of this section to stretch our approximation a bit further, including larger redshifts and, by extension, a second-order correction to the aforementioned law.
\par Let us now approximate $a(t)$ in a neighborhood of today
\begin{equation*}
    a(t) = a(t_{0}) + \left(\parder{a}{t}\right)_{t_{0}}(t-t_{0}) + \frac{1}{2}\left(\parparder{a}{t}\right)_{t_{0}}(t-t_{0})^{2} + \text{o}\left((t-t_{0})^{3}\right)
\end{equation*}
so that (setting $a(t_{0}) = 1$)
\begin{equation}
    a(t) = a_{0}\left[1 + H_{0}(t-t_{0})-\frac{1}{2}q_{0}H_{0}^{2}(t-t_{0})^{2} + \text{o}\left((t-t_{0})^{3}\right)\right]
    \label{eq:a_expansion-second}
\end{equation}
where we've defined 
\begin{equation}
    H_{0} = \left(\frac{\dot{a}(t)}{a(t)}\right)_{t_{0}} \quad q_{0} = -\left(\frac{\ddot{a}(t)a(t)}{\dot{a}^{2}(t)}\right)_{t_{0}}
\end{equation}
\par We also know that
\begin{equation*}
    z = \frac{a(t_{0})}{a(t_{e})}-1 = -1 + \frac{1}{1-H_{0}(t_{0}-t)-\frac{1}{2}H_{0}^{2}q_{0}(t_{0}-t)^{2}}
\end{equation*}
so we can find an approximate relation for the redshift Taylor expanding
\begin{equation*}
    z \approx H_{0}(t_{0}-t_{e})+\left(1+\frac{q_{0}}{2}\right)H_{0}^{2}(t_{0}-t_{e})^{2}
\end{equation*}
\par Remember that at first order we found the relation $z(t) = H_{0}(t_{0}-t_{e})$, which we can plug into last equation introducing corrections only to the third order
\begin{equation}
    H_{0}(t_{0}-t_{e}) \approx z(t) -\frac{1}{2}(q_{0}+2)z^{2} +\text{o}(z^{3})
\end{equation}
\par Now we'll turn to investigate how all of this enters into the definition of proper distance
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{r}_{s} =  \int_{0}^{r_{s}}\frac{\diff{r}}{\sqrt{1-kr^{2}}} = \int_{t_{e}}^{t_{0}}\frac{\diff{t}}{a}
\end{equation}
where we're considering a null, radial geodesic for the FLRW metric (\ref{eq:FRW}). We can plug in the second order expansion for $a(t)$
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{r}_{s} = \frac{1}{a_{0}}\int_{t_{e}}^{t_{0}}\diff{t}\left[1+H_{0}(t_{0}-t)+\frac{1}{2}H_{0}^{2}q_{0}(t_{0}-t)^{2}\right]^{-1}
\end{equation}
We can neglect the third-order contribution brought by the third term in the right-hand side of the equation and expand once more 
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{r}_{s} \approx \frac{1}{a_{0}}\int_{t_{e}}^{t_{0}}\diff{t}\left[1-H_{0}(t_{0}-t)\right] = \frac{(t_{0}-t_{e})}{a_{0}} - \frac{H_{0}(t_{0}-t_{e})^{2}}{2a_{0}}
\end{equation}
which means that our proper distance is ($c=1$)
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{r}_{s} = \frac{1}{a_{0}}\left[(t_{0}-t_{e})+\frac{1}{2}H_{0}(t_{0}-t_{e})^{2}\right]
\end{equation}
or, in terms of the redshift
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{r}_{s}(t_{0}) = \frac{c}{H_{0}}z\left[1-\frac{(1+q_{0})}{2}z\right]
    \label{eq:properdistance_rs_second}
\end{equation}
We can substitute the expression at second order for the proper distance in the definition of distance luminosity (\ref{eq:luminositydistance}) to find 
\begin{equation}
    d_{L}^{2} = \frac{1}{H_{0}^{2}}\left(z-\frac{1}{2}(q_{0}+2)z^{2}+\frac{z^{2}}{2}\right)^{2}(1+z)^{2}
\end{equation}
which we can expand to quadratic order 
\begin{equation}
    H_{0}d_{L} = -\left[z-\frac{1}{2}(q_{0}+1)z^{2}\right](1+z) = z+z^{2}-\frac{1}{2}(q_{0}+1)z^{2} + \text{o}(z^{3})
\end{equation}
which yields
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    H_{0}d_{L} = z + \frac{1}{2}(1-q_{0})z^{2} + \text{o}(z^{3})
    }
\end{equation}

\subsection{Angular diameter distance}

\par Let's consider a curved, expanding Universe described by the FLRW metric and choose your comoving coordinate system so that the observer is at its origin.
\par Consider a yardstick of constant proper length $l$ perpendicular to the line of sight and at a comoving coordinate distance $r_{e}$. At a time $t_{e}$, the yardstick emitted the light that you observe at time $t_{0}$. The comoving coordinates of the two ends of the yardstick, at the time the light was emitted, were $(r_{e},\theta_{1},\phi)$ and
$(r_{e},\theta_{2},\phi)$.
\par The distance $\text{d}s$ between the two ends of the yardstick, measured at the time $t_{e}$ when the light was emitted, can be found from the FLRW metric
\begin{equation*}
    \text{d}s = a(t_{e})f_{k}(\chi)\diff{\Omega} = a(t_{e})\tilde{r}_{s}\diff{\Omega}
\end{equation*}
where $a(t_{e})\tilde{r}_{s}$ is the physical (proper) distance of the emitter at the time of emission. We can set $\text{d}s = l$ and define
\begin{equation}
    d_{A} := \frac{\diff{s}}{\diff{\Omega}} = \tilde{r}_{s}\frac{a(t_{e})}{a_{0}}a_{0} = \frac{\tilde{r}_{s}a_{0}}{(1+z)}
\end{equation}
from which we conclude
\begin{equation}
    d_{A} = \frac{a_{0}f_{k}(\chi)}{1+z} = \frac{d_{L}}{(1+z)^{2}}
    \label{eq:angulardistance}
\end{equation}
You can also show that 
\begin{align*}
    H_{0}d_{A} &= \left[z-\frac{1}{2}(1-q_{0})z^{2}+\text{o}(z^{3})\right](1-2z+\text{o}(z^{2}))\\
    &= z-\frac{1}{2}(3+q_{0})z^{2}+\text{o}(z^{3})
\end{align*}
which is the Hubble law as a function of the angular distance.
\par We can define the apparent brightness $B$ as 
\begin{equation}
    B = \frac{l}{\Omega} = \frac{L}{4\pi d_{L}^{2}}\frac{d_{A}^{2}}{S}
    \label{eq:apparentbrightness}
\end{equation}
where $l$ is the apparent luminosity, $\Omega$ is the angular extension, and $S$ is the physical extension of the source. We can further manipulate the definition introducing the absolute luminosity per unit area, which depends only on the intrinsic characteristic of the source 
\begin{equation}
    B = \frac{\mathcal{L}}{4\pi}\left(\frac{d_{A}}{d_{L}}\right)^{2} = \frac{\mathcal{L}}{4\pi}(1+z)^{-4}
\end{equation}
Suppose that the redshift we observe were produced by some absorbing medium between us and the object. In an Euclidean Universe
\begin{equation}
    d_{L} = d(1+z)^{1/2}
\end{equation}
while $d_{A} = d$, and so 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d_{A}}{d_{L}} = \frac{1}{(1+z)^{1/2}} \implies B \propto (1+z)^{-1}
\end{equation}
but unfortunately observations are telling us that $B \propto (1+z)^{-4}$, thus allowing us to discard the scenario in which the redshift is wholly produced by absorbing media (rather than an expanding Universe).

\subsection{Particle Horizon}

Consider a photon emitted by some source at time $t_{e}$, for a light path $\text{d}s = 0$. The maximum comoving distance the photon might have travelled to present day is
\begin{equation}
   a^{-1}(t)\diff{t} = \frac{\diff{r}}{\sqrt{1-kr^{2}}} \implies \int_{0}^{t_{0}}\frac{\diff{t}}{a(t)} = \int_{0}^{d_{H}}\frac{\diff{r}}{\sqrt{1-kr^{2}}}
\end{equation}
For a single component Universe, the expression for $a(t)$ is simple
\begin{equation}
    a(t) \propto t^{n} \quad n = \frac{2}{3(1+w)}
\end{equation}
and the integral is well defined provided that $w<-1/3$ in the EoS of the Cosmological fluid. The maximum (physical) distance travelled by a photon is then defined as 
\begin{equation}
    d_{\text{max}} = R_{H}(t_{0}) = a(t_{0})\int_{0}^{t_{0}}\frac{\diff{t}}{a(t)} = a(t_{0})\tilde{r}_{\text{max}}(t_{0})
\end{equation}
Such distance is commonly known as \emph{particle horizon} and represents the maximum distance from which we might have possibly received information from the past via electromagnetic waves. For a flat Universe ($k=0$)
\begin{equation}
    \diff{t} = \frac{\diff{x}}{H_{0}xE(x)} \implies d_{\text{max}}(t_{0}) = \int_{0}^{1}\frac{\diff{x}}{H_{0}xE(x)}
\end{equation}
while for a "standard" Universe
\begin{equation}
    R_{H}(t) = a(t)\int_{t_{i}}^{t}\frac{\diff{t'}}{a(t')} \quad a(t)\propto t^{n}
\end{equation}
which is also equal to 
\begin{equation}
    R_{H}(t) = t^{n}\left[\frac{1}{1-n}t^{1-n}\right]^{t}_{t_{i}} \approx \frac{t}{1-n}
\end{equation}
We might relate this last result to the Hubble radius $H^{-1}$, obtaining
\begin{equation}
    \frac{H^{-1}}{R_{H}} = \frac{1-n}{n}
\end{equation}
but please note that this last equation holds only for a simple cosmological model with a single dominating component.

\subsubsection{Event Horizon}

We define the \emph{event horizon} as the maximum distance at which an object can be so that a signal emitted by that same object reaches us at a future infinite. Fixed an emission time $t_{\star}$
\begin{equation}
    \int_{t_{\star}}^{\infty}\frac{\diff{t}}{a(t)} = \int_{0}^{r_{\star}}\frac{\diff{r}}{\sqrt{1-kr^{2}}}
\end{equation}
If $r_{s} > r_{\star}$, the event will never be observed, meaning there could be regions of spacetime that are impossible to "communicate" with. To see if the integral can actually be finite, you will usually check \marginpar{We're assuming a flat Universe $k=0$ for which $$ a(t) = A e^{Ht}$$}
\begin{equation}
    r_{\star} = -\frac{1}{AH}e^{-Ht}\Big|^{\infty}_{t_{\star}} < \infty
\end{equation}
For example, if the EoS has $w>-1/3$, $r_{\star}\to \infty$ and the horizon is not properly defined.

\subsection{Flatness Problem}

Consider a Universe with vanishing Cosmological Constant $\Lambda = 0$ which is also such that Einstein's equation is \emph{always} valid, also at $t=0$. If we define
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{k} = \Omega_{0} = 1-\Omega = \frac{\rho_{c}-\rho}{\rho_{c}} = -\frac{k}{a^{2}H^{2}}
\end{equation}
Now assume that $k\neq 0$ and $\Omega_{0}$ can evolve independently from the dominant contribution to the cosmological fluid. If
\begin{itemize}
    \item MDU: $a(t)\propto t^{2/3}$ and $H \propto a^{-3/2} \implies \Omega_{0}\sim a \sim t^{2/3}\sim \Gamma^{-1}$;
    \item RDU: $a(t)\propto t^{1/2}$ and $H \propto a^{-2} \implies \Omega_{0}\sim a^{2} \sim t \sim \Gamma^{-2}$.
\end{itemize}
Going back in time has the effect of decreasing the value of $\Omega_{0}$. Through studies of the CMB (see Chapter \ref{ch:v} for more), the Planck collaboration constrained the value of $\Omega_{0}$, which was found to be
\begin{equation}
    |\Omega_{0}(t_{0})| < 0.05
\end{equation}
At the time of matter-radiation equality, the spatial density parameter was 
\begin{equation}
    \Big| \frac{\Omega_{0}(t_{eq})}{\Omega_{0}(t_{0})}\Big|^{-1} = \frac{T_{eq}}{T_{0}} \sim 10^{4} \implies |\Omega_{0}(t_{eq})| < 10^{-4}
\end{equation}
Similarly, if we extrapolate backward to the time of Big Bang nucleosynthesis, at $a_{BBN} \approx 3.6\cdot 10^{-9}$
\begin{equation*}
    |1-\Omega_{BBN}|<7\cdot10^{-16}
\end{equation*}
and the distance from unity would only decrease if we went even further back in time. Setting then those orders of magnitude as initial conditions imply a \emph{huge fine-tuning problem}.
\par There is no known reason for the density of the Universe to be so close to the critical density, and this appears to be an unacceptably strange coincidence in the view of most astronomers. This is the so called \emph{Flatness problem}.
\par Let us define 
\begin{equation}
    x = \log\left(\frac{a}{a_{0}}\right) \implies H = \der{\ln(a)}{t} \implies \diff{t} = \frac{1}{H}\diff{\ln(a)} = \frac{1}{H}\diff{x}
\end{equation}
and recall Friedmann equation (\ref{eq:friedmann1}) for a perfect fluid
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\ddot{a}}{a} = \frac{4\pi G}{3}\rho (1+3w) \implies 2q = (1+3w)\Omega(t)
\end{equation}
and 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}} = -(1+q)
\end{equation}
Putting all of this to work, we gather 
\begin{equation}
    \der{\Omega_{0}}{t} = \frac{k}{a^{3}}\frac{2\dot{H}}{H^{3}} + \frac{k}{H^{2}}\frac{2\dot{a}}{a^{3}} = -2\Omega_{0}H\left(1+\frac{\dot{H}}{H^{2}}\right) = -2\Omega_{0}H(1-(1+q))
\end{equation}
If we want the final result to be independent from the particular cosmology that we've chosen \marginpar{Just in case you have forgotten, with the superscript we're referring to the derivative with respect to the conformal time.}
\begin{align}
    & 2\Omega_{0}' = 2\Omega_{0}q \\
    & 2q = (1+3w)\Omega = (1+3w)(1-\Omega_{0})
\end{align}
which means 
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
        \Omega_{0}' = (1+3w)(1-\Omega_{0})\Omega_{0}
    }
\end{equation}
This tells us that if $\Omega_{0}\approx 0$, the stationary point is unstable if $1+3w > 0$! Similarly, $\Omega_{0}\approx 0$ is a stable attractor if $1+3w < 0$, which means $w < -1/3$.
\par The flatness problem is thus resolved if we postulate a Universe that accelerates for enough time. Such period of time is called \emph{inflationary epoch} or just \emph{inflation}.
\par If during the inflation $H \sim \text{const.}$ then 
\begin{equation}
    \Omega_{k} \approx -\frac{k}{a^{2}H^{2}}
\end{equation}
Let $t_{i}$ be inflation's starting time and $t_{f}$ the ending time
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\Omega_{0}(t_{f})}{\Omega_{0}(t_{i})} = \left(\frac{a_{f}}{a_{i}}\right)^{2} = e^{-2N}
\end{equation}
where we defined $N = \ln(a_{f}/a_{i})$ the number of $e$-folds of the inflationary epoch. We want that at the end of inflation $|\Omega_{0}(t_{f})|\sim 10^{-61}$, while we assume a strongly non-flat Universe at the beginning of inflation $|\Omega_{0}(t_{i})|\sim 1$. For our requirements to be met, the Universe should expand a number of $e$-folds such that
\begin{equation}
    e^{-2N} \approx 10^{-60} \implies N \geq 60
\end{equation}
During inflation, then, the comoving Hubble radius is decreasing 
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
    \frac{1}{aH} \downarrow \implies |\Omega_{0}| = \frac{|k|}{a^{2}H^{2}}\downarrow
    }
    \label{eq:solvedfp}
\end{equation}
thus resulting in a quasi-de Sitter Universe with $p=-\rho$ and $H \sim \text{const.}$

\subsection{Horizon Problem}

Another problem remains to be addressed. We know (and we'll see) that photons decouple from ordinary matter at $z\approx 1100$ (decoupling/last scattering). Currently, within our particle horizon, we observe a roughly homogeneous temperature distribution, which is that of the CMB. So far so good.
\par From last scattering until present day, the Universe was dominated from non-relativistic matter, hence 
\begin{equation}
    R_{H}(t_{LS})\approx H_{LS}^{-1} = H_{0}^{-1}\left(\frac{a_{LS}}{a_{0}}\right)^{3/2}\approx R_{H}(t_{0})\left(\frac{a_{LS}}{a_{0}}\right)^{3/2}
\end{equation}
But here comes the problem. Since 
\begin{quote}
    "\emph{Scales scale with the scale factor}"
\end{quote}
\marginpar{This is essentially the corresponding particle wavelength at last scattering.}
\begin{equation} 
    \lambda_{H_{0}}(t_{LS}) = \frac{R_{H}(t_{0})}{1+z_{LS}}
\end{equation}
we get a gargantuan problem $R_{H}(t_{LS}) \ll \lambda_{H_{0}}(t_{LS})$, which can be rewritten in a perhaps clearer form 
\begin{equation}
    \left(\frac{\lambda_{H_{0}}(t_{LS})}{R_{H}(t_{LS})}\right)^{3} = \left(\frac{a_{LS}}{a_{0}}\right)^{-3/2} \approx 10^{5}
\end{equation}
which means that $10^{5}$ volumes are \emph{not} in causal connection!
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/hp.png}
    \caption{The horizon problem in the conventional Big Bang model. All events that we currently observe are on our past light cone. The intersection of our past light cone with the spacelike slice labelled CMB corresponds to two opposite points in the observed CMB. Their past light cones don’t overlap before they hit the singularity, $a = 0$, so the points appear never to have been in causal contact. The same applies to any two points in the CMB that are separated by more than 1 degree on the sky. Credits: \cite{Baumann_2022}.}
    \label{fgr:hp}
\end{figure}
\par This is a huge problem in order to find a sensible explanation for the impressive homogeneity of the CMB (since after decoupling photons are no longer interacting with matter).
\par Perhaps you won't be surprised when I say that inflation comes to the rescue this time as well.
\par During inflation $H_{i}^{-1}\approx \text{const}$ while structures would continue to grow until they would eventually cross the horizon. If we assume a large enough inflationary period, the scales within which we observe the current homogeneity could be able to enter once more into the horizon because they're exponentially suppressed.
\par At the end of an à la de Sitter inflation (but French-sounding) the particle horizon was 
\begin{equation}
    R_{H} = a_{f}\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\frac{\diff{t}}{a(t)}
\end{equation}
with 
\begin{equation}
    a(t) = a(t_{i})\exp(H_{i}(t-t_{i}))
\end{equation}
meaning that 
\begin{equation}
    \frac{a_{f}}{a_{i}} = \frac{a(t_{f})}{a(t_{i})} = \exp(H_{i}(t_{f}-t_{i}))
\end{equation}
We can also invert the expression
\begin{equation}
    a(t_{i}) = a(t_{f})\exp(-H_{i}(t_{f}-t_{i}))
\end{equation}
and define the number of $e$-folds like we've done earlier $N = H_{i}(t_{f}-t_{i})$. This allows us to write 
\begin{equation}
    R_{H}(t_{f}) = a(t_{f})\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}} \frac{\diff{t}}{a(t_{f})}\exp(H_{i}(t_{f}-t))
\end{equation}
If we assume that the particle horizon at last scattering is such that 
\begin{equation}
    R_{H}(t_{LS}) = a(t_{LS})\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{LS}}\frac{\diff{t}}{a(t)} \approx a(t_{LS})\int_{t_{i}}^{t_{f}}\frac{\diff{t}}{a(t)}\exp(H_{i}(t_{f}-t))
\end{equation}
we conclude 
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
        R_{H}(t_{LS}) = \frac{a_{LS}}{a(t_{f})}\frac{1}{H_{i}}\left[\exp(H_{i}(t_{f}-t_{i}))-1\right] = \frac{a_{LS}}{a(t_{f})}\frac{1}{H_{i}}(e^{N}-1)
    }
\end{equation}
\par Our problem is solved if $R_{H}(t_{LS}) > \lambda_{H_{0}}(t_{LS})$
\begin{equation}
    \frac{a_{LS}}{a(t_{f})}\frac{1}{H_{i}}(e^{N}-1) > \frac{1}{H_{0}(1+z_{LS})} = \frac{a_{LS}}{a_{0}H_{0}}
\end{equation}
hence if
\begin{equation}
    \boxed{
        e^{N} \gtrsim \frac{a(t_{f})H_{i}}{a(t_{0})H_{0}}
    }
    \label{eq:solvedhp}
\end{equation}
How do we interpret this last result? All scales we observe today had always been causally connected during the whole expansion of the Universe.
\par For the sake of completeness, let's prove that the conditions to solve both the flatness and horizon problems are the same.
\begin{equation}
    |\Omega_{0}(t_{0})| = \frac{|k|}{a^{2}H_{0}^{2}} \quad |\Omega_{0}(t_{f})| = \frac{|k|}{a_{f}^{2}H^{2}(t_{f})}
\end{equation}
This implies \marginpar{Recall that during inflation the Hubble constant is not changing.}
\begin{equation}
    |\Omega_{0}(t_{0})|a^{2}H_{0}^{2} = |\Omega_{0}(t_{f})|a_{f}^{2}H^{2}(t_{f}) = |\Omega_{0}(t_{f})|a_{f}^{2}H_{i}^{2}
\end{equation}
but 
\begin{equation}
    \Big|\frac{\Omega_{0}(t_{f})}{\Omega_{0}(t_{i})}\Big| = e^{-2N} \implies |\Omega_{0}(t_{0})| = e^{-2N}|\Omega_{0}(t_{i})|\left(\frac{a_{f}^{2}H_{i}^{2}}{a_{0}^{2}H_{0}^{2}}\right)
\end{equation}
If we set $|\Omega_{0}(t_{i})| \approx 1$ \marginpar{The request $|\Omega_{0}(t_{0})| < 1$ purely relies on observational evidence.}
\begin{equation}
    |\Omega_{0}(t_{0})|\approx e^{-2N}\left(\frac{a_{f}^{2}H_{i}^{2}}{a_{0}^{2}H_{0}^{2}}\right) < 1 \iff e^{N} > \frac{a(t_{f})H_{i}}{a_{0}H_{0}}
\end{equation}
which is the condition that solves the horizon problem.

    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/hpinflation.png}
    \caption{Inflationary solution to the horizon problem. The comoving Hubble sphere shrinks during inflation and expands during the conventional Big Bang evolution (at least until dark energy takes over at $a \approx 0.5$). Conformal time during inflation is negative. The spacelike singularity of the standard Big Bang is replaced by the reheating surface, i.e. rather than marking the beginning of time it now corresponds simply to the transition from inflation to the standard Big Bang evolution. \subsection{Hubble Radius}

The Hubble radius we've defined as $R_{H}\sim H^{-1}$ for both MDU and RDU is different from that we have in an accelerated Universe. The Hubble radius at a given time $t$ is roughly equal to the distance travelled by a photon during an "expansion time," (the time needed for the scale parameter to double) starting from said time $t$.
\par As a consequence, two particles that are separated by $\lambda > H^{-1}(t_{0})$ will also be causally connected if the distance that separated them at some earlier time was $\lambda(t) < H^{-1}(t)$
\par Say that $\tilde{R}_{H}(t_{0})$ is the distance crossed by a photon in $t \to t_{\star}$ so that $a(t_{\star}) = 2a(t)$ (so, an expansion time). By virtue of its own definition
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{R}_{H}(t) = a(t_{\star})\int_{t}^{t_{\star}} \frac{\diff{t}}{a(t)} = a(t_{\star})\int_{a(t)}^{a(t_{\star})}\frac{\diff{a}}{a^{2}H} = a(t_{\star})\int_{a(t)}^{2a(t)}\frac{\diff{a}}{a^{2}H}
\end{equation}
\par Consider a small expansion time (respect to the age of the Universe) so that approximately $H(t)\sim\text{const.}$ Then
\begin{equation}
    \tilde{R}_{H}(t) = \frac{1}{H(t)}
\end{equation}
but if now $\lambda < H_{0}^{-1}$, while $\lambda > H(t_{LS})^{-1}$ at an earlier time, then there must necessarily exist an epoch during which $\lambda$ is growing faster than $H^{-1}$
\begin{equation}
    \frac{\text{d}}{\text{d}t}\left(\frac{\lambda}{H^{-1}}\right) > 0
\end{equation}
but if $\lambda \sim a$, this implies $\ddot{a} > 0$, hence the Universe must be accelerating, hence the necessity of inflation.
\par What we surmise, then, is that the comoving Hubble radius $(aH)^{-1}$ mush be increasing right after the BBN, but for earlier times it must be decreasing, as pictorially shown in Fig.\ref{fgr:inflation}.
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{img/inflationscales.png}
    \caption{Scales of cosmological interest were larger than the Hubble radius until $a \approx 10^{-5}$ (where today is at $a(t_{0}) = 1$). However, at very early times, before inflation operated, all scales of interest were smaller than the Hubble radius and therefore susceptible to microphysical processing. Notice the symmetry of the inflationary solution. Scales just entering the horizon today, 60 $e$-folds after the end of inflation, left the horizon 60 $e$-folds before the end of inflation. Credits: \cite{Baumann_2022}.}
    \label{fgr:inflation}
\end{figure}
\par The existence of an epoch of accelerated expansion corresponds to having $p < -\rho/3$ and violates the \emph{strong energy condition}.
\par Several cosmologists began to postulate the necessity of an inflationary period starting from the end of 1970s. Remarkably, Starobinski{\v{i}} \cite{1979JETPL..30..682S} noted that quantum corrections to GR should be important for the early Universe. The solution to the Einstein field equations in the presence of curvature squared terms, when the curvatures are large, leads to an effective cosmological constant. Therefore, he proposed that the early universe went through an inflationary de Sitter era, but never mentioned that such inflationary epoch could solve the problems intrinsic of the standard hBBN model.
\par Only two years later, Guth \cite{1981PhRvD..23..347G}, and the following month still Guth and Weinberg \cite{1981PhRvD..23..876G}, showed that the horizon and flatness problems could be solved if we allowed the existence of an extraordinary supercooling in the early history of the Universe to temperatures 28 or more orders of magnitude below the critical temperature for some phase transition. But "Unfortunately, the scenario seems to lead to some unacceptable consequences, so modifications must be sought."
\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering 
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{img/guthinflation.jpg}
    \caption{In simple inflationary models, the Universe at early times is dominated by the potential energy density of a scalar field, $\phi$. Red arrows show the classical motion of $\phi$. When $\phi$ is near region (a), the energy density will remain nearly constant, $\rho\approx \rho_{f}$, even as the Universe expands. Moreover, cosmic expansion acts like a frictional drag, slowing the motion of $\phi$: Even near regions (b) and (d), $\phi$ behaves more like a marble moving in a bowl of molasses, slowly creeping down the side of its potential, rather than like a marble sliding down the inside of a polished bowl. During this period of "slow roll," $\rho$ remains nearly constant. Only after $\phi$ has slid most of the way down its potential will it begin to oscillate around its minimum, as in region (c), ending inflation. Credits: \href{https://ned.ipac.caltech.edu/level5/March05/Guth/Guth1.html}{this website}.}
\end{figure}
\par In short, they were postulating the existence of a scalar field $\phi$. The associated potential $U(\phi)$ had to present a local maximum where the phase transition occurred (so at the beginning of inflation), and a local minimum for $\phi$ where $U(\phi)\approx \text{const.} = \Lambda$, hence a metastable de Sitter phase during which tunnel effect occurred towards true vacuum. This was the so-called \emph{old inflation}.
\par What had gone wrong with Guth's and Weinberg's model was solved only one year later by Andrej Dmitrievič Linde \cite{1982PhLB..108..389L}, \cite{1982PhLB..116..335L} and independently by Andreas Albrecht and Paul Steinhardt \cite{1982PhRvL..48.1220A} in a model named \emph{new inflation} or \emph{slow-roll inflation}.
\par In this model, instead of tunneling out of a false vacuum state, inflation occurred by a scalar field rolling down a potential energy hill. When the field rolls very slowly compared to the expansion of the Universe, inflation occurs. However, when the hill becomes steeper, inflation ends and reheating can occur.
